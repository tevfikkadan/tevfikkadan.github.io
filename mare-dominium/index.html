Concept · Maritime Geopolitics

MARE
DOMINIUM

Mare Dominium describes a condition in which a state or power can establish effective control over maritime space through the combined effects of armed power, technology and strategic geography — without necessarily possessing formal sovereignty or ownership over the sea.
Conceptual framework developed by Tevfik Kadan · 2026
The central question

For centuries, the freedom of the seas was not only a legal principle. It also reflected a physical reality: oceans were extraordinarily difficult to observe, police and control continuously.

Mare Dominium asks what happens when that material condition begins to change. Persistent surveillance, satellites, unmanned platforms, networked sensors, long-range precision weapons and control of strategic chokepoints increasingly allow states to see farther, strike farther and impose costs across maritime spaces that were once too vast to dominate effectively.

The issue is therefore not simply whether international law continues to recognise freedom of navigation. The deeper question is whether technological and military power can create control in practice even where legal freedom remains on paper.

01 · POWER

Armed capacity

Long-range precision strike, naval and coastal systems, mines and other means capable of denying or constraining access.

02 · TECHNOLOGY

Persistent awareness

Satellites, radar, unmanned systems, sensors and networks that reduce the opacity traditionally associated with the sea.

03 · GEOGRAPHY

Strategic position

Straits, islands, ports, enclosed seas and chokepoints where geography magnifies the effects of surveillance and armed power.

From Mare Liberum to Mare Dominium

Mare Liberum is historically associated with the principle that the seas should remain open to navigation and use rather than be appropriated as territorial possessions. Its rival tradition, Mare Clausum, defended claims to maritime dominion and exclusion.

Mare Dominium is not simply a return to Mare Clausum. It distinguishes legal ownership from effective control. A maritime area may remain legally open while the practical freedom to operate within it becomes increasingly dependent on the tolerance, capability or counter-capability of powerful actors.

Mare LiberumLegal freedom

The sea as a space open to navigation and use beyond sovereign territorial limits.

Mare ClausumClaimed closure

The historical idea that parts of the sea may be subject to possession, dominion or exclusion.

Mare DominiumEffective control

Control achieved materially through power, technology and geography, even without formal ownership.

Why technology matters

The scale of the oceans has always imposed severe costs on continuous control. A navy may defeat another fleet without being able to monitor every merchant vessel, submarine, unmanned platform or movement across millions of square kilometres.

That constraint is changing incrementally rather than disappearing overnight. Improvements in remote sensing, autonomous systems, data fusion and precision weapons can compress the distance between detection and action. The decisive threshold is not a single invention; it is the point at which surveillance, identification, decision and enforcement become sufficiently persistent and affordable to alter behaviour at sea.

This is why Mare Dominium should be understood as a developing condition, not a claim that the world's oceans have already been conquered.

“History has not yet recorded the conquest of the seas.” The key word is yet.

Chokepoints and the geography of control

Effective control does not have to begin with the entire ocean. Geography creates places where a relatively limited area can influence a disproportionately large flow of trade, energy and naval movement.

Straits and narrow seas therefore provide some of the clearest laboratories for the concept. The Strait of Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb, the Turkish Straits and other maritime bottlenecks illustrate a general principle: control over the sea's narrow gates can generate strategic effects far beyond the physical area being controlled.

In this sense, the future of maritime power may depend not only on who possesses the largest fleet, but also on who can combine geography with persistent sensing and credible denial capabilities.

Control without ownership

The distinction is central. Mare Dominium does not require a state to declare an ocean its sovereign territory. Nor does it predict that the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea will simply disappear.

Instead, it focuses on the possible divergence between juridical status and operational reality. Ships may retain a legal right to navigate while facing surveillance, targeting, exclusion risks, insurance costs, electronic interference or military pressure that changes whether and how that right can actually be exercised.

The concept therefore offers a framework for examining maritime competition in a world where law remains important but material capability increasingly determines the conditions under which legal freedoms are used.
